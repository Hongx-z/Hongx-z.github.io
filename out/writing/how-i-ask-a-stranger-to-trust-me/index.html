How I Ask a Stranger to Trust Me

A working method, written down mostly so I stop forgetting it. Two visits before the camera comes out, and a set of rules about what I will and will not do to get a good scene.

4 Aug 2025·3 min read

Contents

  • Rule two: consent is a process, not a form
  • What I will not do
  • The part nobody warns you about

Every documentary maker has a method and almost nobody writes theirs down, which is why the craft reproduces itself through apprenticeship and rumour. This is mine. It is not better than anyone else's. It is just explicit.

Rule one: two visits before the camera

The first visit is for talking and the second is for sitting quietly. On the first visit I do not take notes, and I do not bring anything that looks like equipment — not even a small recorder, because people read a phone face-down on a table as a recorder and they are usually right.

On the second visit I bring a notebook and use it sparingly. The goal is to be boring. Boring is the entire technique. A person who is bored of you has stopped performing for you, and that is when the room gets honest.

The camera does not make people open up. It makes them close, and then, if you wait long enough, it makes them forget. My job is to shorten the waiting by already being furniture.

By the third visit, when the camera finally appears, the interesting thing is how little changes. People who have been bored of you for two weeks will let you film them eating breakfast, because you have already been there for breakfast.

Rule two: consent is a process, not a form

I use a written release, because I have to. But the paper is the least important part. What matters is that the subject understands four things, and that I have said all four out loud in words they use, not words I use:

  1. What the film is about, as best I know it at this point — which is honestly not much.
  2. Where it might end up: a festival, a website, a classroom, or nowhere.
  3. That they can stop at any point, including after the shoot, including after the edit.
  4. That they will see the cut before it goes anywhere.

Point four costs me scenes. I have lost a woman describing her own eviction, and I have lost a man at the moment he started crying, because both of them changed their minds once they saw themselves. I would rather have the films and not the trust. I have made my choice and I make it again every time, which is the only way I know that I have actually made it.

What I will not do

  • I will not shoot someone in a state they cannot consent from. Drunk, exhausted past a certain point, mid-crisis. If the scene is good and the person is not, I put the camera down and lose the scene. This has happened three times.
  • I will not use the word "just" in a request. "Can you just say that again" is the most coercive sentence in this trade.
  • I will not promise an outcome I do not control. No "this will get you attention". Festival programmers are strangers to me too.
  • I will not keep rolling after "stop". Including the ones that are clearly reflexive. Especially those.

The part nobody warns you about

The method works, and the working is the problem. Being furniture in someone's life for six weeks makes you a fixture in it. When the film is done I leave, because that is what the job is, and I have watched this go badly in both directions: subjects who felt abandoned, and filmmakers who never left and slowly became the thing they were documenting.

I now say this at the start, in plain language, before anything else: I will be in your life intensely for a few months, and then much less, and that is not a comment on you. It is a strange thing to say to a stranger. It has never once been received badly.

If you are starting out and you take one thing from this: the access is not the hard part. Anybody can get access once. The hard part is being the kind of person who gets access twice.
