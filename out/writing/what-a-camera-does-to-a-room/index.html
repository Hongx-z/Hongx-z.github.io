What a Camera Does to a Room

The first twenty minutes are always performance, the next twenty are always self-consciousness, and what happens after that is the only reason to keep the camera running.

30 Mar 2024·3 min read

Contents

  • The three phases
  • Why the equipment matters less than you think
  • The practical consequences

There is a shape to every filmed encounter, and after four years of doing this I have started to be able to predict it, which is useful mostly because it stops me panicking during the bad part.

The three phases

Minutes 0–20: performance. The subject is aware of the camera and is being a version of themselves. This version is usually slightly more polite, slightly more articulate and substantially more boring than the real one. If you have ever filmed anything, you know exactly what this looks like and you have probably, at some point, cut it into a film anyway. Do not do that.

Minutes 20–40: self-consciousness. The performance collapses and is replaced by awkwardness. The subject starts editing themselves mid-sentence, apologising, asking whether they should start again. This is the phase where most documentary makers give up and go home, or start directing heavily, and both of those are mistakes.

Minutes 40 onwards: the room is a room again. Somewhere in here — and it genuinely does happen, reliably, every single time — the subject stops monitoring themselves. Their posture changes. Their sentences get shorter and stranger and better. They say something that is not for you.

The first forty minutes are not footage. They are the price of admission, and the price is non-negotiable. You cannot skip it, and anything you get during it is a performance you are going to have to live with.

Why the equipment matters less than you think

There is a whole genre of advice about small cameras being less intrusive. It is mostly true and it is mostly irrelevant. A phone on a table changes a room. So does a notebook. So does the fact that you got out a pen.

What actually determines how quickly the room goes back to normal is not the size of the camera. It is whether you are still paying attention twenty minutes in. A subject who can tell you have stopped listening will re-perform, forever, out of pure social reflex. A subject who can tell you are still listening will eventually stop performing, because performing for someone who is genuinely listening is exhausting and nobody can sustain it.

The equipment is a decoy because it is the thing you can buy.

The practical consequences

If the shape above is right — and I am confident it is, at least for the kind of filming I do — then a few things follow that I did not believe when I started.

  1. Short shoots are structurally incapable of depth. A two-hour shoot gets you forty minutes of unusable material and eighty minutes of something. This is why the vast majority of television feels performed: it is, because there was no time.
  2. The good scene is almost never the one you planned. It is the one after the one you planned, when everyone has relaxed because the planned thing is over.
  3. You must build the waiting into the schedule and defend it. In practice this means shooting a subject for four hours to use eleven minutes, and being unable to explain to a producer why that is not waste.

Which is also why the films take as long as they take. I have not found a way to shorten the forty minutes. I have looked. Every shortcut I tried produced a film that was fluent, competent, and empty.

There is a counter-argument, and it is a good one: some people are extraordinary from the first second, and some rooms are so used to cameras that phase one never happens. Both are real. Both are also rarer than anyone wants to admit, and neither is something you can count on when you are standing in a stranger's kitchen at seven in the morning with a bag you have not finished packing.
