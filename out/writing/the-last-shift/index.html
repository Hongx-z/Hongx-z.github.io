The Last Shift

For twenty-six years the plant ran three shifts a day. On the final night, forty-one people showed up to switch off machines they had run their whole working lives.

18 Nov 2025·3 min read

Contents

  • What a shutdown actually looks like
  • The people who stayed
  • The thing I got wrong

The announcement had come eleven weeks earlier, in a language nobody in the room could parse: consolidation of production capacity. What it meant, in practice, was that the plant would stop at 06:00 on a Friday, and that forty-one people would walk out of it for the last time.

I was allowed in for the final night shift on the condition that I did not get in the way. I got in the way twice.

What a shutdown actually looks like

It is not dramatic. That is the first thing worth saying, because every film about a closing factory shows the same thing: a crane, a siren, a man in a hard hat looking up. What I saw instead was paperwork.

Fatima ran the extrusion line for nineteen years. For her last six hours she stood at a terminal entering machine states — decommissioned, drained, isolated, sealed — into a system that would be archived and never read again. She had done this every night for nineteen years, in the sense that she had entered states into it. She had never once entered the word decommissioned.

"You know what the strange part is? The machine doesn't know. It's been waiting for me to press start for nineteen years. It'll be waiting tomorrow."

At 03:40 the compressed-air line was bled down. This is the loudest thing that happens during a factory shutdown, and it happens over about ninety seconds. Forty years of pressure leaving a building through a valve the size of your wrist. Everyone stopped and listened to it. Nobody said anything. It was, by a wide margin, the most emotional thing I have ever witnessed at work.

The people who stayed

Three of the forty-one had turned down transfers to the other site. I asked each of them why, and none of them gave me the answer I expected.

  • Fatima said the new plant was a ninety-minute commute and she was two years from a pension she did not want to lose.
  • Dmitri, who maintained the boiler, said the other site's boiler was electric and he did not trust it. He said this with total sincerity and would not elaborate.
  • A woman who asked me not to use her name said she had applied for the transfer and been told no, and then repeated, four times in the space of ten minutes, that she had never wanted to go anyway.

I have thought about that fourth answer more than anything else from that night. It is not a lie exactly. It is what a person says when the alternative is admitting they were sorted.

The thing I got wrong

I went in with a thesis: that a shutdown is a small death, and that the people inside it would experience it that way. That thesis was wrong, and I had to throw away about two-thirds of my notes on the second night.

What the night actually was, for most of the people there, was a party with a job in the middle of it. Somebody brought a cake. Somebody else brought a speaker. Between 01:00 and 04:00 there was dancing, briefly, in a room with a floor rated for forklifts.

The grief is real. It just is not where you expect to find it, and it arrives on a delay of about four months. I have kept in touch with four of the forty-one. Two of them told me, in the spring, that they had started dreaming about the plant.

The plant is still standing. The land was sold to a logistics company, which intends to demolish it and build a distribution centre on the footprint. I drove past in July. The lights in the west wing were on, which is impossible, because the power was cut in December.

It was almost certainly a security light. I have decided not to find out.
