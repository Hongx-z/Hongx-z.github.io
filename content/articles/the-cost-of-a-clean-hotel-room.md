The number that matters is twenty-two minutes. That is the target cleaning time for a standard double room in the chain I spent three weeks in, and it is not a secret — it is printed on a card in the housekeeping office, next to a laminated diagram of the correct order to do things in.

Twenty-two minutes for a room that took a guest four minutes to make messy. I want to explain why that number is what it is.

## What twenty-two minutes buys

The room rate is ninety. Of that, housekeeping labour is budgeted at roughly three fifty. Out of that three fifty comes a wage, and out of the wage comes a person, and the person has a body, and the body is the part the spreadsheet does not model.

Here is what twenty-two minutes actually requires:

- **No stairs.** A floor with a service lift break-even is around twenty-eight.
- **No turns.** Stripping a bed is fast; carrying linen down a corridor is not.
- **A trolley within four metres.** Every metre away from the door costs about six seconds, twice.
- **Nothing unusual in the room.** A sofa bed is plus six minutes. A guest who has cooked is plus four. A guest who has left a dog in the room is plus twelve and a conversation.

None of that is in the twenty-two. All of it happens, most days, in most rooms.

> I asked one housekeeper what she would change if she could change one thing. She said: "The beds. Make the beds lighter." She had been doing this for eleven years and that was the whole answer. Not pay, not hours, not the supervisor. The beds.

## The part that is about bodies

By the sixth day of shadowing — I worked a real shift, badly, and got fired from it at the end, which is its own story — the thing that had stopped being abstract was the repetition.

A housekeeper on a full floor does about fourteen rooms. That is fourteen times bending to a bed base, fourteen times pushing a vacuum under a desk, fourteen times reaching behind a toilet with a cloth. The motions are identical and the loading is asymmetric: it is always the same shoulder, always the same wrist, always the same lumbar flexion. The industry injury statistics are not surprising once you have watched it. What is surprising is that anybody is surprised.

The second thing is **the clock**, and this one I had not anticipated at all. The cleaning time is measured. The housekeeper knows it is measured. This produces a specific and visible thing: a person working at a pace that is not their natural pace, for eight hours, and being able to tell you, at any moment, roughly how many minutes behind they are.

Three of the nine women I worked alongside could tell me their deficit to the minute, mid-shift, without checking anything. I found that genuinely upsetting and I have not entirely worked out why.

## What I could not write

The piece I filed was about the twenty-two minutes. The piece I could not write was about the other twenty-two, which is what remains when you subtract the paid time from the realistic time, and which is made up, every day, by people deciding to do a slightly better job than they are paid to do.

That is not a heroic framing. It is a subsidy. It is the thing the business model is standing on, and it is invisible in every document I was given across three weeks.

---

The chain has since raised the target to twenty-three minutes and described this, in a press release, as an investment in staff wellbeing. I have read the release four times. It is not wrong, exactly. It is just the smallest possible unit of true.
